# Housing, Mobility & Neighborhood Affordability Package

**Draft for review, October 2026.** This is a working draft, not an official City of Minneapolis document.

This package revises the ideas in `possible_legislation.md`. Each zoning item is written as an amendment to the current Minneapolis Code of Ordinances, Title 20 (Zoning Code), as published through Municode Supplement No. 72 (ordinances through January 2026). Items the City can't adopt by ordinance are written as Council resolutions instead: some need state action, and some are Public Works policy or budget decisions.

**How to read the amendments.** Following the City's drafting convention, ~~struck text~~ is deleted and <ins>underlined text</ins> is added. Section numbers link to the current text in the zoning browser.

### What changed from the original ideas

- **Single-stair reform** moved to Resolution G. Minnesota has one statewide building code that cities can't amend. Also, the International Building Code is the restrictive standard (single stair only up to 3 stories), so "align with international standards" was backwards.
- **Fee waivers** narrowed to City zoning fees (Bill F). The regional sewer charge (SAC) is set by the Metropolitan Council, so a state request is in Resolution G.
- **Storefront reuse** rewritten as an amendment to the existing adaptive-reuse section (545.40). Most of the idea is already law, but it requires a conditional use permit.
- **Daycare** removed from the corner-store list. Child care centers are already permitted in every Urban Neighborhood district.
- **Micro-delivery hubs** dropped from zoning. Package delivery uses are already allowed in commercial and production districts, so this is a curb-management pilot (noted in Resolution H).
- **Transit upzoning** rewritten as a built form "floor" near stations (Bill D). The code already has Transit 10–30 districts; the gap is where they're mapped.
- **"0 parking maximums"** dropped. Parking minimums have been zero since 2021 and the code already has maximums.
- **"Tax credit"** replaced with tax abatement, which is a power the City actually has (Minn. Stat. §§ 469.1812–469.1815).
- **Street items** moved to a resolution (H). They aren't zoning, and Lake Street and several other corridors are Hennepin County roads.
- **The impact claims** in the original matrix were toned down. See [Combined effects](#combined-effects).

## Bill A — Missing Middle Floor Area Parity

**An ordinance amending Title 20, Chapters 540 and 545 of the Minneapolis Code of Ordinances relating to Zoning Code: Built Form Overlay Districts and Use Regulations.**

### Purpose

The 2040 Plan legalized up to three units on residential lots, but Table 540-2 still caps floor area for 1–3 unit buildings at 0.5 in the Interior 1 and Interior 2 districts. On a typical 5,000 sq ft lot that is about 2,500 sq ft above grade. A triplex is about 830 sq ft per unit, too small for family-sized homes. About 37 triplexes were built citywide in the first two and a half years.

Current height (2.5 stories) and lot coverage (45%) limits already permit a building of about 1.1 times the lot area. Raising the floor area cap is therefore the only change needed to make 3–6 unit buildings workable without changing building height or footprint.

### The City Council of the City of Minneapolis do ordain as follows:

**Section 1.** That Table 540-2, Maximum Floor Area Ratio, in Section 540.110 of the above-entitled ordinance be amended to read as follows (only the amended rows are shown):

| Built Form Overlay District | Primary Zoning District Category | Maximum Floor Area Ratio (Multiplier) |
|---|---|---|
| Interior 1 | UN, RM | <ins>Residential buildings with 3 to 6 units: 1.1</ins><br>~~All uses except Institutional and Civic Uses: 0.5~~<br>Institutional and Civic Uses: 0.8<br><ins>All other uses: 0.5</ins> |
| Interior 1 | All other districts | Residential buildings with 1-~~3~~<ins>2</ins> units: 0.5<br><ins>Residential buildings with 3 to 6 units: 1.1</ins><br>All other buildings: 1.4 |
| Interior 2 | UN, RM | Residential buildings with 1-~~3~~<ins>2</ins> units: 0.5<br><ins>Residential buildings with 3 or more units: 1.1</ins><br>All other buildings: 0.8 |

### Possible effects

- **Housing supply:** makes 3–6 unit buildings financially workable on standard lots across Interior districts. Expect a meaningful but gradual increase, likely dozens to low hundreds of units per year at first. Land cost, teardown cost and interest rates will still limit how many lots redevelop.
- **Unit size and families:** at 1.1, a triplex on a 5,000 sq ft lot could have units of about 1,800 sq ft, enough for 2–3 bedroom family homes.
- **Neighborhood form:** height and coverage limits don't change, but new buildings would use more of the allowed envelope. They would be noticeably bulkier than the 1920s–1950s houses next door, so expect concerns about massing, shade, and loss of yard trees.
- **Teardowns:** higher allowed floor area raises the value of land under small, older homes. That may speed demolition of some modest homes, which are often the most affordable existing housing.
- **Affordability incentive (unintended effect):** the existing 0.1 per unit affordable housing premium (540.230(b)) becomes less valuable when the base floor area rises from 0.5 to 1.1. Consider raising that premium in the same ordinance.
- **Interior 3 protection:** the table note keeps Interior 3 buildings with 4+ units at their current 1.4. Without it, a flat 1.1 rule could reduce what is allowed today.
- **City finances:** more units per lot raise property tax base without new streets or pipes. Sewer and water laterals may need upsizing for 4–6 units, at the builder's cost.
- **Legal and process:** allowing 4–6 units in UN1 likely needs a 2040 Plan amendment and Metropolitan Council review, because the Plan's Urban Neighborhood guidance contemplates up to three units on most interior lots. The 2024 state law that shields comprehensive plans from environmental-review lawsuits reduces, but doesn't remove, litigation risk.
- **Who is affected:** owners of standard residential lots, small builders, and renters and buyers seeking family-sized units. Neighbors of redeveloping lots see the most change.
- **Limited new homes on standard lots (from the [housing simulator](sim.html)):** Bill A keeps the 9,000 sq ft (Interior 1) and 7,500 sq ft (Interior 2) minimum lot sizes for buildings with 4 or more homes. On a typical 5,000 sq ft lot it therefore allows a bigger triplex, not more homes. To house 50,000 more people, Bill A needs about 9% fewer redeveloped lots than current code (6,383 vs 7,022), but each new home costs more ($747K vs $577K) because the triplexes are larger. See Option A-2 below.

### Option A-2 — Minimum lot size for 4–6 home buildings (optional amendment)

This optional section would be added to Bill A as a new Section 4, renumbering the effective-date section. It lets 4–6 home buildings go on standard lots in Interior 1 and Interior 2, matching the 5,000 sq ft and 40 ft minimums Interior 3 already uses.

| Interior 2 | ~~7,500~~ <ins>5,000</ins> | ~~50~~ <ins>40</ins> |
| Interior 3 | 5,000 | 40 |

#### What the simulator shows

Same lots, same order, "most likely to redevelop," at twice today's building pace (300 projects a year):

| To house 50,000 more people | Current code | Bill A as drafted | Bill A + Option A-2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lots that redevelop | 7,022 | 6,383 (−9%) | **4,617 (−34%)** |
| Homes per redeveloped lot | 3.8 | 4.2 | **5.8** |
| Cost per new home | $577K | $747K | **$529K** |
| Total cost | $15.5B | $20.1B | **$14.2B** |
| Years to build | 23 | 21 | **15** |

When redevelopment is spread evenly across neighborhoods, the pattern holds: 7,970 lots under current code, 7,516 under Bill A, and 5,256 with Option A-2 (−34%).

#### Reasons against changing the minimum lot size

**Building and site**

1. **Bulk next to houses.** On a 5,000 sq ft lot, a 4–6 home building at 1.1 floor area would be about 5,500 sq ft above grade, compared with 1,200–2,000 sq ft for a typical older house. Height (2.5 stories) and side yards (5 ft) don't change, so the building fills more of its envelope, with more shade and less privacy for neighbors.
   - *Counterpoint:* those limits are the same ones a large single-family house already gets, and design standards can shape how the bulk is arranged.
2. **Crowding on the lot.** Six homes need more trash and recycling carts and screening (550.90), one bike parking space per home (Table 555-2), landscaping on 20% of the open site (550.680), plus mailboxes, utility meters, and snow storage. A larger lot makes room for these.
   - *Counterpoint:* these are design problems with known solutions, and the same rules already work on Interior 3's 5,000 sq ft lots.
3. **Street parking.** With no parking minimums, a 6-home building may add 4–8 cars to the street, which matters most during snow emergencies.
   - *Counterpoint:* most Minneapolis blocks have alleys, and parking unbundling (Bill C) lowers car ownership.
4. **Trees and stormwater.** Larger buildings mean more mature tree loss and more runoff, even within the 60% impervious surface limit (Table 540-24).

**Market and neighborhood**

5. **Teardown and displacement pressure.** Making nearly every lot eligible for 4–6 homes raises land values broadly. Investors may target the least expensive houses first, in North Minneapolis, Phillips, and parts of South Minneapolis, where many owners are lower-income or people of color. Today's larger minimum forces builders to combine lots, which slows this and steers apartment buildings to larger and corner lots.
   - *Counterpoint:* the simulator's "most likely" scenario concentrates redevelopment in Southwest Minneapolis, where land makes up the largest share of property value, and displacement can be addressed directly with anti-displacement tools.
6. **Loss of older, modest homes.** Older small houses are often a neighborhood's most affordable housing. New units typically rent or sell for more than the homes they replace.
7. **Smaller real-world payoff.** The simulator assumes every redeveloping lot builds the most zoning allows. In practice few lots redevelop in any year, and land, construction, and interest costs decide most projects.
8. **Financing changes at 5 or more homes.** Buildings with 1–4 homes qualify for standard residential mortgages. Buildings with 5 or more need commercial loans, which are harder to get for small builders and owner-occupants. This is an argument for capping standard lots at 4 homes rather than for keeping the larger minimum.

**Legal and political**

9. **2040 Plan consistency.** Allowing 4 or more homes on standard lots across Interior 1 and Interior 2 likely goes beyond the 2040 Plan's guidance for those areas. That would require a comprehensive plan amendment, Metropolitan Council review, and new legal risk. The 2040 Plan spent years in court under the Minnesota Environmental Rights Act before the 2024 state fix.
10. **Political risk to the whole bill.** This change affects every block in Southwest, Nokomis, and Northeast. It could build enough opposition to sink Bill A's other provisions, such as the floor area increase.

#### Middle-ground options

- **4 homes on 5,000 sq ft lots, 5–6 homes on 7,500 sq ft or more.** Keeps small projects within residential mortgage financing and limits bulk on small lots.
- **Corner lots or alley lots only.** Corner lots have two street frontages for entrances, trash, and light; alley lots handle trash and parking off the street.
- **Near frequent transit only**, for example within ¼ mile of a high-frequency bus or rail line. Pairs naturally with Bill D.
- **Phase it in.** Apply the change to Interior 2 first, review results after three years, then decide on Interior 1.
- **Pair it with anti-displacement measures.** Fund preservation and right-of-first-offer programs in the same package so the land value effect is addressed from the start.

*Evidence to weigh:* Interior 3 already allows 4 or more homes on 5,000 sq ft lots, so Minneapolis has local experience to study. Portland, Oregon's Residential Infill Project allows up to four homes on most residential lots, and six when some are affordable. Confirm current details before citing it.

## Bill B — Corner Commercial & Storefront Reuse

**An ordinance amending Title 20, Chapter 545 of the Minneapolis Code of Ordinances relating to Zoning Code: Use Regulations.**

### Purpose

No retail, food, or office uses are allowed in the UN1, UN2, or UN3 districts today. Former corner groceries and storefronts can be reused under section 545.40, but almost all reuse requires a conditional use permit: a $780+ application, a public hearing, and months of uncertainty. This ordinance:

- allows small corner businesses on corner lots by right, under strict operating standards; and
- makes reuse of existing storefronts permitted, with no conditional use permit.

### The City Council of the City of Minneapolis do ordain as follows:

**Section 1.** That Table 545-1, Uses Allowed, in Section 545.100 be amended by adding the following row in the Commercial use group, under General Retail Sales and Services:

| Uses | UN1 | UN2 | UN3 | RM1 | Use Standards |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| <ins>Corner commercial establishment</ins> | <ins>2P</ins> | <ins>2P</ins> | <ins>2P</ins> | <ins>2P</ins> | <ins>+</ins> |

*Drafting note: the use table expresses floor area limits in thousands of square feet. "2P" allows up to 2,000 sq ft; the 1,800 sq ft cap in the original idea is set in the use standards below.*

**Section 2.** That Section 545.210, Commercial use standards, be amended by adding a new subsection as follows:

<ins>(l) Corner commercial establishments. Corner commercial establishments shall be subject to the following standards:</ins>

<ins>(1) Location. The establishment shall be located on a corner lot, with its principal customer entrance facing a street. Not more than one (1) corner commercial establishment shall be located on each corner of an intersection, and not more than two (2) per intersection.</ins>

<ins>(2) Allowed uses. The establishment shall be limited to the following: grocery store; bakery; coffee shop; restaurant without on-sale liquor; general retail sales; personal services; dry cleaning drop-off without on-site processing; office; art studio; and small goods repair, such as bicycles, shoes, electronics, and small appliances.</ins>

<ins>(3) Size. The gross floor area of the establishment shall not exceed one thousand eight hundred (1,800) square feet, and the establishment shall be located on the ground floor. Dwelling units may be located above or behind the establishment.</ins>

<ins>(4) Hours. The establishment shall not be open to the public between 10:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m.</ins>

<ins>(5) Operations. Drive-through facilities, outdoor amplified sound, and outdoor storage are prohibited. Deliveries shall occur between 7:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. by vehicles not exceeding a single-unit truck. Refuse and recycling containers shall be fully screened.</ins>

<ins>(6) Signs. Signs shall comply with the standards for commercial uses in Table 560-1, Specific Standards for Signs in the Urban Neighborhood and RM1 Districts.</ins>

**Section 3.** That Section 545.40, Adaptive reuse, be amended as follows. The same changes are made to subsections (c)(4) and (d)(3) for the UN2 and UN3 districts.

(b) UN1 District. In the UN1 District, additional authorized uses shall be limited to the following and shall only be allowed by conditional use permit unless otherwise indicated:

(4) The following nonresidential uses provided the individual size of each of these nonresidential uses shall not exceed five thousand (5,000) square feet, except as authorized by section 545.40(g):

a. Art gallery.

b. Art studio.

c. Clinic, medical or dental.

d. Community provisions facility.

e. Convent, monastery, or religious retreat center.

f. Grocery store.

g. Offices.

<ins>h. Food and beverages uses, excluding bars and nightclubs.</ins>

<ins>i. General retail sales and services uses, excluding uses listed separately in Table 545-1, Uses Allowed.</ins>

Minimum lot dimension requirements shall not apply to these nonresidential uses provided the lot area shall not be reduced below nine thousand (9,000) square feet or reduced further if less than nine thousand (9,000) square feet. <ins>The uses listed in this subsection shall be permitted uses, and shall not require a conditional use permit, when the use occupies floor area that was originally constructed for nonresidential use and the use complies with the hours, operations, and sign standards of section 545.210(l). A conditional use permit shall be required for any use that does not meet these conditions or that exceeds the size limits of this subsection.</ins>

**Section 4.** This ordinance takes effect thirty (30) days after publication.

### Possible effects

- **Daily trips:** some short car trips for coffee, groceries and errands become walks. The effect on citywide driving (VMT) will be modest. Corner businesses need foot traffic to survive, and most homes are already within about half a mile of a commercial corridor. The biggest benefit is likely in neighborhoods far from corridors.
- **Number of businesses:** expect a slow trickle, likely tens of new corner businesses citywide over several years, not hundreds. The storefront-reuse change will probably produce results faster, because the buildings already exist.
- **Cost and time savings:** removing the conditional use permit saves at least the $780–$1,300 permit fee and roughly 2–4 months of hearing time per storefront reuse. It also removes the risk of denial, which matters most for small, first-time business owners.
- **Neighbor concerns:** potential noise, deliveries, trash and on-street parking. The hours, delivery and size standards are meant to address these. Without a public hearing, neighbors lose a formal chance to object to individual projects.
- **Other licensing still applies:** food businesses still need Health Department licenses. Liquor stays governed by Title 14, and this ordinance doesn't allow on-sale liquor.
- **Property taxes:** a corner commercial space changes part of a parcel to commercial classification, which can raise that owner's property taxes.
- **Housing interaction:** owners could convert ground-floor housing to commercial, a small loss of units. Allowing dwellings above and behind the business limits this.
- **Who is affected:** corner-lot owners, small business owners, residents within walking distance, and neighbors adjacent to new businesses.

## Bill C — Residential Parking Unbundling

**An ordinance amending Title 20, Chapter 555 of the Minneapolis Code of Ordinances relating to Zoning Code: Off-Street Parking, Loading, and Mobility.**

### Purpose

Minneapolis eliminated parking minimums in 2021, but many buildings still include parking in the rent. Car-free tenants then pay for stalls they don't use; underground stalls cost roughly $40,000–$60,000 each to build. Today, unbundling counts as a 1-point optional strategy in travel demand management plans (Table 555-11), and those plans are only required for buildings with 50 or more units. This ordinance makes unbundling a requirement for new buildings with 10 or more units.

### The City Council of the City of Minneapolis do ordain as follows:

**Section 1.** That Chapter 555, Article III be amended by adding a new Section 555.250 to read as follows:

<ins>**555.250. Unbundled residential parking.**</ins>

<ins>(a) Applicability. This section applies to any building containing ten (10) or more dwelling units, for which a building permit for new construction or for a change of use to residential is issued after the effective date of this section.</ins>

<ins>(b) Separate lease or sale. Off-street parking spaces for residential units shall be leased or sold separately from the rental or purchase price of the dwelling units. No tenant or buyer shall be required to rent or buy a parking space as a condition of renting or buying a dwelling unit.</ins>

<ins>(c) Price disclosure. The monthly rent or sale price of parking spaces shall be stated separately from the price of dwelling units in all leases, listings, and advertising.</ins>

<ins>(d) Right of first refusal. Residents of the building shall have the first right to rent available parking spaces. Spaces not rented by residents may be leased to non-residents.</ins>

<ins>(e) Exemptions. This section shall not apply to:</ins>

<ins>(1) Accessible parking spaces provided to residents who require them, which shall be offered at a price no greater than other spaces.</ins>

<ins>(2) Dwelling units where a federal, state, or local affordable housing program prohibits separate parking charges, to the extent of that prohibition.</ins>

<ins>(3) Parking provided in a garage or driveway that serves a single dwelling unit and is accessible only from that unit.</ins>

<ins>(f) Compliance. Compliance with this section shall be a condition of the certificate of occupancy and of the rental license for the building.</ins>

**Section 2.** That the "Unbundling and pricing of parking" row of Table 555-11, Travel Demand Management Strategies, in Section 555.1350 be amended as follows:

| Strategy | Points | Standards |
|---|---|---|
| Unbundling and pricing of parking | 1 | a. In a residential use subject to a travel demand management plan, parking spaces for residential units shall be leased or sold separately from the rental or purchase price of the housing units<ins>, and shall be offered on a daily or hourly basis in addition to monthly. Compliance with section 555.250 alone shall not qualify for points</ins>. b. In a nonresidential use subject to a travel demand management plan … *(unchanged)* |

**Section 3.** This ordinance takes effect one hundred eighty (180) days after publication, to allow time to update leasing documents and rental license procedures.

### Possible effects

- **Household costs:** car-free and one-car households in new buildings avoid paying for stalls they don't use. Expect savings to appear partly and over time, not as an immediate $150–$250 rent cut. Landlords set unit rents at what the market will bear, so some of the parking cost will stay in base rents, especially at first.
- **Car ownership:** research consistently finds that bundled parking increases car ownership. Separate pricing should modestly lower car ownership and driving among new-building residents.
- **Construction:** over time, developers will build fewer stalls once they see how many tenants pay for them separately. This lowers construction costs per unit.
- **Street parking:** some residents may park on the street to avoid paying for a stall. Expect pressure in areas near popular buildings and requests for residential permit parking. Pricing stalls reasonably and leasing extras to non-residents helps.
- **Scope:** the requirement applies only to new buildings and conversions. Existing buildings aren't covered, so the citywide effect builds over years.
- **Affordable housing:** most income-restricted programs already restrict mandatory parking fees. The exemption avoids conflicts with program rules.
- **Administration and legal:** enforcement relies on the certificate of occupancy and rental licensing. Because this regulates lease terms, the City Attorney should confirm whether it belongs in the Zoning Code or in Title 12 (Housing). Placing it in both may be safest.
- **Who is affected:** developers and landlords of buildings with 10+ units, car-free renters (they benefit), and car-owning renters (they see separate parking charges).

## Bill D — Station Area Built Form Minimums

**An ordinance amending Title 20, Chapter 540 of the Minneapolis Code of Ordinances relating to Zoning Code: Built Form Overlay Districts.**

### Purpose

The code already has the tools for transit-oriented density. The Transit 10–30 built form districts allow 10–30 stories, have floor area ratios of 5 to 10, and have lower parking maximums. The gap is where they're mapped: some lots near METRO light rail and bus rapid transit stations are still mapped Interior 1–3 or Corridor 3–4. Rather than remapping lot by lot, this ordinance sets a minimum built form for lots close to stations.

### The City Council of the City of Minneapolis do ordain as follows:

**Section 1.** That Chapter 540, Article I be amended by adding a new Section 540.50 to read as follows:

<ins>**540.50. Station area minimum built form.**</ins>

<ins>(a) Definitions. For the purposes of this section, "METRO station" means a light rail transit station or bus rapid transit station of the METRO system that is in operation, or for which construction funding has been committed, on the date a complete land use application is submitted.</ins>

<ins>(b) Inner station area. For any zoning lot located wholly or partly within six hundred sixty (660) feet of a METRO light rail transit station platform, the floor area ratio, height, lot coverage, and impervious surface coverage standards of the BFT10 Transit 10 Built Form Overlay District shall apply where they are less restrictive than the standards of the mapped built form overlay district.</ins>

<ins>(c) Outer station area. For any zoning lot located wholly or partly within one thousand three hundred twenty (1,320) feet of a METRO station platform, the floor area ratio, height, lot coverage, and impervious surface coverage standards of the BFC6 Corridor 6 Built Form Overlay District shall apply where they are less restrictive than the standards of the mapped built form overlay district.</ins>

<ins>(d) Residential uses. On zoning lots subject to this section in the UN1 or UN2 Districts, multiple-family dwellings shall be permitted uses, subject to the standards of section 545.250.</ins>

<ins>(e) Exceptions. This section shall not apply to:</ins>

<ins>(1) Landmarks and properties within historic districts designated under Title 23, Heritage Preservation.</ins>

<ins>(2) The standards of the SH Shoreland, FP Floodplain, and MR Mississippi River Corridor Critical Area Overlay Districts, which shall continue to apply.</ins>

<ins>(f) Map. The zoning administrator shall maintain a map of the areas subject to this section, and shall update it when METRO stations open or receive construction funding.</ins>

**Section 2.** This ordinance takes effect thirty (30) days after publication.

### Possible effects

- **Housing capacity:** potentially the largest increase in capacity in this package, concentrated where frequent transit already runs. Actual construction will depend on market strength around each station, which varies widely by corridor.
- **Transit and driving:** residents near stations drive less and use transit more, and more riders strengthen the case for frequent service. This is the bill with the clearest link to lower driving (VMT).
- **Neighborhood change:** blocks near stations that are now mostly houses could see 4–6 story buildings. Within 660 ft of light rail, buildings could reach 10 stories. That is a large visual change, and expect strong neighbor reaction.
- **Displacement:** rising land values near stations may pressure existing renters, especially along corridors in North Minneapolis and Phillips. Pair this bill with anti-displacement tools such as tenant opportunity-to-purchase and preservation funding.
- **Bus line uncertainty:** BRT stations can be relocated or dropped, unlike rail. The "construction funding committed" definition limits changes to stations that are very likely to exist.
- **Legal and process:** likely needs a 2040 Plan amendment and Metropolitan Council review. It is more blunt than a planner-led remapping and may produce odd results on individual blocks. A remapping study could be done in parallel.
- **Who is affected:** property owners within a quarter mile of stations, transit riders, residents near stations, and Metro Transit, which gains ridership.

## Bill E — Surface Parking Conversion

**An ordinance amending Title 20, Chapter 550 of the Minneapolis Code of Ordinances relating to Zoning Code: Development Standards; and a resolution establishing a property tax abatement policy for surface parking conversions.**

### Purpose

Large surface parking lots along commercial corridors (for example on Lake Street, Nicollet Avenue, and West Broadway) are prime sites for housing. Today, residential projects with 20 or more units need site plan review by the City Planning Commission, with a public hearing. This bill gives qualifying conversions administrative review and offers time-limited property tax abatement on the City's share of taxes.

### Part 1 — Ordinance

**Section 1.** That Table 550-1, Buildings and Uses Subject to Site Plan Review, in Section 550.510 be amended by adding the following row:

| Buildings and Uses |
|---|
| <ins>Any residential or mixed-use building constructed on a zoning lot where at least fifty (50) percent of the lot area was used as a surface parking lot for at least five (5) years before application, located in the Corridor 4, Corridor 6, Transit 10, Transit 15, Transit 20, Transit 30A, Transit 30B, or Core 50 Built Form Overlay District. The site plan review application shall be reviewed administratively, regardless of the number of dwelling units, if the project does not include any other land use application requiring a public hearing.</ins> |

### Part 2 — Resolution (summary)

*Resolved by the City Council of the City of Minneapolis:*

1. That the City adopts a policy, under Minn. Stat. §§ 469.1812–469.1815, to consider abatement of the City's share of property taxes on the added value of qualifying surface parking conversions, for up to ten (10) years.
2. That projects receiving abatement must include at least ten (10) percent of units affordable at 60% of area median income, consistent with the City's Unified Housing Policy.
3. That staff are directed to seek matching abatement agreements with Hennepin County and Minneapolis Public Schools for their shares.

### Possible effects

- **Speed:** removing the public hearing shortens approval for large corridor projects by roughly 2–3 months and reduces approval risk. That matters for financing.
- **Housing:** helps the projects the market already supports along strong corridors. It won't create demand on weak corridors, where abatement matters more.
- **City revenue:** the City gives up new tax revenue on the added value for up to ten years. Because these lots now pay low taxes, the City's existing revenue isn't reduced.
- **Public input:** less formal neighborhood input on the largest buildings. Site plan standards still apply, and staff still review them.
- **Business impact:** removing parking can hurt existing businesses that depend on it, so expect pushback from retailers and shopping center owners. The conversion is voluntary for the owner.
- **Who is affected:** owners of large surface lots, corridor businesses, residents of new housing, and taxing jurisdictions.

## Bill F — Infill Zoning Fee Relief

**An ordinance amending Title 20, Chapter 525 of the Minneapolis Code of Ordinances relating to Zoning Code: Administration and Procedures.**

### The City Council of the City of Minneapolis do ordain as follows:

**Section 1.** That Section 525.230, Fees, be amended by adding a new subsection (d) to read as follows:

<ins>(d) Infill housing fee waiver. The fees in Table 525-1, Fees, shall be waived for applications for a residential building with two (2) to six (6) dwelling units on a zoning lot that:</ins>

<ins>(1) Has been vacant for at least two (2) years; or</ins>

<ins>(2) Contains a building registered as vacant under Chapter 249 of this Code, or a building ordered demolished as a hazardous or substandard building.</ins>

<ins>The waiver shall not apply to applications for variances, or to fees established outside this zoning ordinance.</ins>

### Possible effects

- **Cost to builders:** saves roughly $475–$2,000 per project. That helps at the margin but rarely decides whether a project is feasible. Larger costs, such as building permits, SAC and utility connections, aren't covered.
- **Where it applies:** most qualifying vacant and condemned lots are in North Minneapolis. It complements existing City programs for City-owned lots, but it does little in Southwest, Nokomis, or Northeast.
- **City budget:** lost fee revenue is likely small, in the tens of thousands of dollars per year. The cost of processing applications shifts to the general fund.
- **Building permit fees:** waiving them would need a separate resolution, because they are set outside Title 20. SAC needs state or Metropolitan Council action, so it is included in Resolution G.

## Resolution G — State Legislative Priorities

*Resolved by the City Council of the City of Minneapolis*, that the City's legislative agenda includes:

1. **Single-stair buildings.** Support amending the Minnesota State Building Code to allow multifamily buildings with a single exit stair up to six (6) stories, with fire-safety safeguards similar to those adopted in Seattle and New York City. The Department of Labor and Industry's code advisory group has reportedly approved a four-story limit for the 2026 code, and bills proposing more (SF 5153 / HF 4545) were pending in 2026.
2. **Bus-lane and bus-stop camera enforcement.** Support state authority for camera enforcement of vehicles blocking transit lanes and bus stops, building on the Minneapolis speed-camera pilot authorized by the Legislature.
3. **Sewer charge (SAC) relief for small infill.** Ask the Metropolitan Council and the Legislature to allow deferral or reduction of SAC for 2–6 unit infill buildings.

### Possible effects

- **Single stair:** lowers costs and makes better apartment layouts possible on small 4–6 story corridor sites. It doesn't matter much for Interior districts, where height is capped at 2.5–3 stories. Fire officials will want strict safeguards.
- **Bus-lane cameras:** faster, more reliable buses on BRT corridors. Expect concerns about surveillance and privacy at the Legislature.
- **SAC relief:** saves small builders thousands of dollars per unit, but shifts costs to other sewer ratepayers across the region.

## Resolution H — Complete Streets & Winter Walkability

*Resolved by the City Council of the City of Minneapolis:*

1. **Protected bikeways.** That the Complete Streets Policy be amended so that City-owned streets with posted speeds of 25 mph or higher, when reconstructed, include physically separated bikeways where the adopted bicycle network calls for them, unless the City Engineer documents why that isn't feasible. That the City ask Hennepin County to adopt the same standard for county roads in Minneapolis, including Lake Street.
2. **Bus lanes.** That Public Works, working with Metro Transit, identify peak-hour bus-only lanes on City-controlled segments of high-frequency corridors, and report to the Council within one year.
3. **Sidewalk snow clearing.** That the City continue and expand the municipal sidewalk clearing pilot, now about $600,000 per year, to cover designated high-pedestrian corridors, routes to METRO stations, and school walk zones. The City Coordinator should report on the full cost by corridor. The City has estimated citywide clearing at about $40 million per year, so a phased network is the realistic path.
4. **Micro-delivery.** That Public Works run a curbside micro-delivery pilot, with cargo-bike loading zones and hub siting, in partnership with delivery companies. No zoning change is needed.

### Possible effects

- **Walking and biking:** safer, year-round routes encourage more short trips without a car, especially for children, older adults and people with disabilities.
- **Bus speed:** dedicated lanes make buses faster and more reliable, but they take away curb parking or travel lanes. Expect business concerns on each corridor.
- **Cost:** protected bikeways add cost to reconstruction projects. A sidewalk clearing network costs millions per year depending on its size and needs a stable funding source.
- **Limits:** the City can only set standards on its own streets. County roads like Lake Street need Hennepin County's agreement.

## Open questions for review

1. **Option A-2:** should Bill A include the 5,000 sq ft minimum lot for 4–6 home buildings, one of the middle-ground versions, or neither? This choice decides most of Bill A's effect on housing supply.
1. **Floor area for 2-unit buildings:** should two-family dwellings also get more than 0.5 in Interior 1 and 2, or is the 3+ unit focus intentional?
2. **Floor area exclusions:** should basement and half-story exclusions (540.120) extend to 4–6 unit buildings? That would allow more floor area beyond 1.1.
3. **Affordable premium:** should the affordable housing premium (540.230(b)) increase from 0.1 per unit to keep its value?
4. **Unbundling threshold:** is 10 units the right cutoff for Bill C, or should it start at 20 or 50 like the travel demand rules?
5. **Station areas:** should Bill D use straight-line distance (as drafted) or walking distance? Walking distance is more accurate but harder to administer.
6. **Packaging:** should Bills A and D be paired with anti-displacement measures in the same package?
